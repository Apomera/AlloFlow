Research for all twelve species

Common octopus — Selects and modifies dens to reduce predation exposure. On soft sediment, observed shelters include holes, cavities beneath stones and empty shells; solid shelter material can limit distribution. Source

Greater blue-ringed octopus — Spends much time hidden and camouflaged. Disturbance exposes conspicuous blue rings; the rings reflect light through iridophores and are revealed by muscular action. They are warning displays, not bioluminescent lamps. Source

Mimic octopus — Associated with exposed sand or mud, occupies vacated animal burrows and may burrow itself. The cited species record describes daytime activity and feeding on small fish and crustaceans in sediment, and discusses reported dynamic mimicry. Source

Giant Pacific octopus — Uses tight rocky dens, leaves prey-shell middens nearby and generally hunts at night. Its flexible body can pass through small openings, with the beak imposing a physical restriction. Arms and suckers locate and hold varied prey. Source

Caribbean reef octopus — Field experiments documented use of cavities beneath benthic formations and preference for opaque over translucent artificial dens. An institutional guide describes nocturnal reef foraging, dark crevice shelters and camouflage during movement between refuges. Source

Coconut or veined octopus — Observed transporting coconut shell halves on soft sediment and assembling a shelter later. Transport uses cumbersome stilt-walking; the carried shells do not protect the animal before deployment. Source

Common cuttlefish — Occurs on sandy and muddy shelf substrates. The FAO account describes daytime sand burial and ambush of prey while partly buried. Camouflage and concealment therefore complement the rapid tentacle strike. Source

Hawaiian bobtail squid — The researchers' image caption documents morning burial to hide from predators and emergence at dusk for hunting. This gives burial a different daily role from cuttlefish ambush. Source

Humboldt squid — ROV observations document group foraging and pigment patterns associated with interactions. Researchers propose that signals help squid avoid contact or competition during feeding; the meanings of pattern sequences remain unresolved. Source

Vampire squid — Collects marine snow using two sticky filaments and transfers gathered material toward the mouth. A low-energy feeding strategy suits oxygen-poor midwater. Defense includes arm-web inversion, luminous arm tips and luminous fluid rather than ordinary ink. Source

Dumbo octopus — Expedition video of Grimpoteuthis bathynectes documents deep-sea swimming with large fins and webbed arms. The expedition account explicitly notes how little is known about these animals' sensory biology. Source

Chambered nautilus — Laboratory experiments demonstrate three-dimensional tracking of food odor plumes. Acoustic telemetry documents vertical foraging movements along reef slopes, with activity patterns varying among populations. The rigid external shell distinguishes its movement and defense from flexible octopuses. Source
