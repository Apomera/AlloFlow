Q2. How does antibiotic resistance evolve?
Original answer: Random mutations + antibiotic selection: the few resistant survivors multiply
Original result: Correct on the original quiz
Correct answer: Random mutations + antibiotic selection: the few resistant survivors multiply
Explanation: Mutations are random. When antibiotic is present, susceptible bacteria die and resistant survivors live to reproduce. Selection - not learning, not intention - drives resistance. Antibiotic overuse (and use in animal feed) accelerates the process.

Q4. What is the difference between a bacterium and a virus?
Original answer: Bacteria are cells with their own machinery; viruses are genetic material that needs a host cell to reproduce
Original result: Correct on the original quiz
Correct answer: Bacteria are cells with their own machinery; viruses are genetic material that needs a host cell to reproduce
Explanation: Bacteria are living cells. They have a cell membrane, DNA, ribosomes, and can grow and reproduce on their own (given food). Viruses are not cellular - they are DNA or RNA in a protein shell. To reproduce, they must enter a host cell and hijack the cell's machinery.

Q5. How does a vaccine work?
Original answer: Teaches the immune system to recognize a pathogen before you're infected
Original result: Correct on the original quiz
Correct answer: Teaches the immune system to recognize a pathogen before you're infected
Explanation: A vaccine exposes the immune system to a harmless version of part of the pathogen (an inactivated virus, a protein subunit, or - for mRNA vaccines - instructions to make a viral protein). The immune system makes memory cells that recognize the real pathogen if encountered. Vaccines prevent disease; they don't treat existing infection.

Q6. In sourdough bread, what causes the sour flavor AND the rise?
Original answer: Lactic acid bacteria make the sour; yeast makes the CO₂ that rises the bread
Original result: Correct on the original quiz
Correct answer: Lactic acid bacteria make the sour; yeast makes the CO₂ that rises the bread
Explanation: Sourdough relies on two organisms working together: lactic acid bacteria (Lactobacillus) make the sour by fermenting sugars into lactic acid, and wild yeast makes CO₂ that lifts the dough. Commercial yeast bread skips the bacteria - it rises fast but doesn't develop the sour flavor or the same digestibility.

Q8. Why did Fleming's 1928 penicillin discovery take 14+ years to become a usable drug?

Original result: Correct on the original quiz
Correct answer: Mass production of pure penicillin required years of work by Florey, Chain, Heatley, and a whole team
Explanation: Fleming's observation in 1928 was important but he could not produce or purify penicillin in any quantity. Howard Florey, Ernst Chain, Norman Heatley, and colleagues at Oxford figured out purification and mass production starting in 1939. US wartime industrial scale-up made it widely available by 1944. Most "discoveries" become real through the work of many.

Q10. What feeds your gut microbiome?
Original answer: Dietary fiber from plants
Original result: Correct on the original quiz
Correct answer: Dietary fiber from plants
Explanation: Most of your gut microbes ferment fiber that you cannot digest, producing short-chain fatty acids that nourish the colon lining. A diverse plant-rich diet feeds a diverse microbiome. Refined carbohydrates and sugar are absorbed in the small intestine - they don't reach the gut microbes. Probiotic pills are a tiny dose; food is the main intervention.

Q12. Which organelles have bacterial ancestors, according to endosymbiotic theory?
Original answer: Mitochondria and chloroplasts
Original result: Correct on the original quiz
Correct answer: Mitochondria and chloroplasts
Explanation: Mitochondria and chloroplasts evolved from bacterial ancestors that became integrated into host cells in separate ancient events. Their DNA and bacterial-like ribosomes are among the evidence. Mitochondria occur in most eukaryotic cells; chloroplasts occur in photosynthetic cells of plants and many algae. These are organelles, not independent microbes living in every cell.

Q13. Genetically, which group are fungi closest to?
Original answer: Animals
Original result: Correct on the original quiz
Correct answer: Animals
Explanation: Despite looking more plant-like (rooted, can't move), fungi are evolutionary cousins of animals. We share a common ancestor about 1.1 billion years ago - more recent than the split with plants. Fungi have chitin cell walls (like insect exoskeletons), not cellulose. They cannot photosynthesize. They digest externally.

Q14. How many DOMAINS of cellular life do biologists recognize today?
Original answer: Three (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya)
Original result: Correct on the original quiz
Correct answer: Three (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya)
Explanation: Carl Woese's 1977 ribosomal RNA work showed Archaea are as different from bacteria as bacteria are from us. The three-domain framework (Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya) is the foundation of modern biology. Archaea were originally classified with bacteria because they look similar under the microscope, but they are biochemically distinct.

Q15. Why do antibiotics that target the bacterial cell wall (like penicillin) NOT work against viruses?
Original answer: Viruses do not have cell walls - they have no equivalent target
Original result: Correct on the original quiz
Correct answer: Viruses do not have cell walls - they have no equivalent target
Explanation: Viruses have no cell wall, no ribosomes, no DNA replication machinery of their own - they hijack the host's. They have NO equivalent target for any antibiotic. This is why prescribing antibiotics "just in case" for a viral cold or flu is useless (and accelerates resistance for the bacteria that ARE in your body).
