## Introduction

Generative artificial intelligence is increasingly used to assist with professional documentation, including psychological and psychoeducational report writing. In a recent national descriptive study, school psychologists who used AI for report writing most commonly applied it to bounded tasks such as rewriting sections and generating recommendations, and nearly all reported editing AI-generated content before use (Farmer et al., 2026). Related experimental work found that licensed psychologists generally favored human-written psychological reports over reports generated by ChatGPT-4, although most effects were small and relative performance varied by report component (Lockwood et al., 2025). Together, these findings establish both practical interest and a need for direct validation of AI-generated report content.

NotebookLM differs from a general-purpose chatbot because its user-facing workflow is designed to generate responses grounded in a selected collection of sources. Document grounding may reduce unsupported generation, but it does not guarantee accurate interpretation. Existing NotebookLM evaluations have reported markedly different performance across tasks. A journalism study found at least one hallucination in 13% of NotebookLM outputs, with many errors reflecting unsupported interpretive characterization rather than obvious fabrication (Hagar et al., 2025). NotebookLM achieved 86% complete staging accuracy across 100 synthetic lung-cancer cases and 95% reference-location accuracy, yet still made errors after retrieving relevant information (Tozuka et al., 2024). It performed more consistently on constrained dental-guideline questions (Dufey-Portilla et al., 2026) and less reliably on a judgment-intensive risk-of-bias task (Beber et al., 2025).

Psychoeducational report generation combines several demands represented unevenly in this literature: exact transcription of scores, preservation of attribution and time, integration of discrepant sources, communication of uncertainty, professional interpretation, and linkage of recommendations to identified needs. A report may contain no spectacular fabrication while still misrepresenting a score, erasing disagreement, overstating certainty, omitting a limiting fact, or attaching a citation that does not support the resulting claim. Consequently, a useful evaluation must distinguish factual support, external correctness, completeness, citation entailment, and plausible professional consequence.

The present study uses synthetic psychoeducational records paired with explicit gold-standard ledgers to evaluate NotebookLM at the atomic-claim and report levels. The primary objective is to estimate the proportion of claims that are unsupported or contradicted by the provided sources under a prespecified standard-prompt workflow. Secondary objectives address consequential errors and omissions, numerical and attribution accuracy, citation behavior, uncertainty handling, reproducibility, and the effect of a source-constrained guardrail prompt. An embedded prospective audit additionally documents how an AI research agent contributed to protocol development, study infrastructure, analysis, and manuscript drafting, together with the human interventions required to make those contributions scientifically acceptable.

### Limitations

- Synthetic cases may not reproduce all ambiguity, social context, or document-quality problems found in practice.
- Results apply to the dated NotebookLM workflow, prompts, source formats, and account configuration tested.
- Human judgments of interpretation and severity may remain variable despite calibration.
- A raw-output benchmark does not model the professional editing reported in typical use.
- The AI research agent contributed to study materials, creating risks of shared assumptions that require independent human validation.
- The embedded process audit lacks a conventional-research comparator unless one is added prospectively.
