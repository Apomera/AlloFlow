# Applied Problem Solving: small usability trial

This is a proposed observation guide. No learner sessions have been conducted. The preview uses an authored scenario and mocked AI; it can test navigation and recovery, but cannot establish the quality of a live model's feedback or learning outcomes.

## Set up

Invite 4–6 learners who reflect the intended age range and varied reading, writing, keyboard, and device needs. Use a familiar lesson and a teacher-reviewed task. Include a narrow screen and keyboard-only session. Use the same task for comparisons, and let learners use their usual accessibility supports.

Allow about 20 minutes per session. Explain: “We are checking the tool. If something is confusing, that helps us improve it.” Observe without showing the path first. Record actions and anonymized notes; recording audio or video is unnecessary for this protocol.

## Tasks to observe

1. **Start independently.** “Tell me what you need to make, then start in your own words.” Record time to first meaningful entry, requests for help, and whether the suggested question is mistaken for their completed work.
2. **Use the lesson.** “Compare two possibilities. Show how a lesson idea supports or challenges one.” Record whether the learner can find the organizer and explain a connection. Ask what “linked to a reviewed lesson fact” means; it should not be understood as proof of their own claim.
3. **Choose an expression format.** “Show your response in a way that works for you.” Observe writing or adding a link with an explanation. Ask what the AI can see at that link. It receives the supplied explanation, not the artifact itself.
4. **Recover a mistake.** After a populated evidence row exists, ask the learner to remove it and recover it. Later, replace a custom question and recover the earlier version. Record whether they find Undo, whether focus returns usefully, and whether other edits survive. Stop and help if they become distressed about lost work.
5. **Check and revise.** “Find a weakness or uncertainty, explain what you checked, then decide what to keep or change.” Observe whether a planned check is confused with an observation. With live AI enabled, inspect the feedback together against the actual lesson; do not treat the model's assessment as ground truth.
6. **Finish and return.** “Review your response. Find one part to improve, make that change, and prepare a copy.” Ask whether they think saving, downloading, and submitting mean the same thing. Recovery is limited to ten recent removals/replacements in the open workspace; reloading clears it.

## Teacher review, separately

Give a teacher a task containing one matching quotation, one missing quotation, and one unmatched quotation. Ask them to explain each status and whether a match verifies the fact. Then give them a vague lesson connection, a task with only cosmetic choices, and an oversized deliverable for the stated time. Run the task-quality review, compare its suggestions to the original task, edit a setting, and check whether the earlier-review notice is understood.

After each session ask: “What took more effort than it should?”, “What did you think the tool had checked?”, and “What would you change first?”

## How to choose the next changes

- Fix lost work, keyboard traps, inaccessible controls, and misleading verification language before cosmetic changes.
- If two learners miss the same action or misread the same status, revise that part and repeat the task with new learners.
- Investigate a repeated delay before adding more instructions; moving or renaming the control may be enough.
- Keep successful independent behaviors intact. Report observations by task and support needs, not as learner ability scores.

This small trial can reveal usability problems. It does not demonstrate educational effectiveness. Any later claim about learning should use a separate study with defined learning measures.
